Analytics for Nutgraf

What a consumer app measures, what Nutgraf should measure in each of seven areas, and three ways to set it up, from nearly free to big-company scale. Written for a PM coming from internal tools: every idea is explained before it is used.

  • RecommendStart the Notebook tier now: about $0 to $5 a month, about six small pull requests, all seven areas at a basic level. Move to Newsroom the day you let other people in. Wire Service is the map of a large consumer app, here for reference.

Three things pay off now: is the product working for you, is anything broken or slow on your phone, and what the AI costs and how good it is. Chapter 1 · Basics

Analytics in plain words

With internal tools, users have to use the tool and you can walk over and ask them. With a consumer app, people decide every day whether to come back, nobody owes you a reason, and you will never meet most of them. Analytics is how you hear them.

Internal tools Consumer app
Did they finish the task? How long did it take? Did they come back tomorrow? Next week?
Adoption is mandated Adoption is earned every day
Feedback: ask the team Feedback: behaviour data, surveys, recordings
Growth: headcount Growth: channels, word of mouth, habit
Cost per user rarely matters Cost per user, and AI cost per user, decides whether the product can exist

How a tap becomes a chart

Every analytics system, from a free tool to Netflix's, has the same five steps.

An event, up close

An event is a small record: what happened, who did it, when, and details called properties. Opening an issue from Today's deck would send this: 

{
  "event": "issue_opened",
  "distinct_id": "u_7f3a91c2",          // who: an opaque ID, never an email
  "timestamp": "2026-10-28T07:42:10+05:30",
  "properties": {
    "issue_id": "18f2c4d0a1b2",         // which: an ID, never the subject
    "source_screen": "today_deck",      // where from
    "rank": 2,                           // position in the list
    "is_top5": true,
    "is_must_read": true,
    "topic": "AI & LLMs",
    "device": "phone",
    "app_version": "d741395"
  }
}
Event
Something that happened, named as a past-tense verb: issue_opened.
Properties
Details about it: which screen, which rank, which device.
User (distinct ID)
Who did it, as an opaque ID. Never the email address.
Session
One visit, usually ended by 30 minutes without activity.
Identify
Joining an anonymous visitor to a known account after sign-in, so your phone and your Mac count as one person, not two.

Two places to capture, and why the route matters

In the app (client-side)

Sees what happens on screen: taps, scrolls, how long a page stayed open. Ad blockers and browser privacy settings block a share of it, and anything in a browser can be faked.

On the server (in the Lambdas)

Sees everything that reaches the API: a rating saved, an ingest run, a Claude call. Cannot be blocked; the truth for anything that changes data. Cannot see taps that never reach the server.

Use both: the server for facts (ratings, saves, AI calls, ingest runs), the app for behaviour (screens, scrolling, swipes). For the app's events, the route decides how many arrive:

Autocapture or a tracking plan

Autocapture

Records every tap and page view with no code. Good for exploring what people click at all. Noisy, and its charts break when a button's text or layout changes.

A tracking plan

A short list of events you name on purpose, each with fixed properties. These are the events you trust in a decision. Kept in the repo as typed code, so a misspelt event fails the build.

Use both: autocapture on, plus about 25 planned events (the plan is in Area 1).

Where the data ends up

Small teams read the data inside the analytics tool. Bigger teams also copy every event into a data warehouse, one large SQL database (BigQuery, Snowflake, Redshift, or files in S3 read with Athena), and treat that copy as the source of truth, because only there can usage be joined with money, support and AI cost. Newsroom starts doing this; Wire Service is built around it.

Chapter 3 · Every tier

Ground rules

These keep the PRD's promise that email content never leaves your AWS account except to Bedrock, and they hold in every tier.

  1. 1No newsletter content in analytics events. Opaque IDs, counts, positions and categories only. Never a subject, headline, summary, sender name, search text or Ask question.
  2. 2Prompts and answers stay in AWS. Every Claude prompt holds newsletter text. Full traces go to Bedrock's logs in your account or a tool you host there; only metadata (feature, model, tokens, cost, time, outcome) may go outside.
  3. 3Recordings hide everything. Session replay masks all text and inputs and never records the newsletter frame.
  4. 4First-party collection. The SDK is bundled from npm, so the CSP keeps script-src 'self'; events go through sandip.dev/nutgraf/ingest, so connect-src 'self' stays. The refresh token sits in localStorage, readable by any script on the page, so the SDK version is pinned and every upgrade is a reviewed pull request.
  5. 5One tracking plan, in code. Names are object_action, past tense, snake case. Properties are typed in packages/core. A new event joins the plan in the pull request that sends it.
  6. 6Dev is not prod. Dev and integration-test traffic go to a separate project.
  7. 7Ask before tracking others. For you alone, no consent banner. When others join: a privacy policy naming every tool, consent for anything non-essential, and their analytics deleted when they delete their account.
Why rule 1 matters twice

gmail.readonly is a restricted scope under Google's API user data policy. Data read through it may only provide the app's user-facing features and may not go to third parties for anything else. Opening the app to the public needs Google's verification and, because Nutgraf stores mail on its servers, an independent security assessment renewed every year. Unverified apps are capped at 100 users. Keeping mail-derived data out of analytics tools keeps that review simple.

Chapter 4 · Focus

The metric that matters most

Consumer teams pick one North Star metric: the number that best captures the value users get. Under it sits a metric tree of inputs a team can move directly.

Proposed North Star: useful reads per reader per week. A useful read is an issue or story you opened and then gave a thumbs up or read to the end. It grows only if you keep coming back, the ranking puts good things first, and the newsletters are worth reading.

Area 1 of 7

Product and behavioural analytics

Who uses it, how often, which features, where do people drop off, and do they come back?

Ideas to know

DAU, WAU, MAU and stickiness
Distinct people active in a day, a week, 30 days. Stickiness is DAU ÷ MAU: 50% means the average user shows up on half the days. Many consumer apps sit around 10 to 25%; a daily-habit product aims well above.
Activation
The moment a new user first gets the value, the "aha". For Nutgraf: mail connected, first issues in, three opened and one rated, within 7 days.
Funnel
The share of people who get through each step of a sequence, in order. It shows exactly where people fall out.
Retention and cohorts
Of the people who started in one week (a cohort), how many came back in week 2, 3, 4. A flattening curve means you have found people who stay.
Feature adoption
The share of active users who used a feature at least once in the period.
Paths
The order of screens people actually take, which is rarely the one you designed.

Area 2 of 7

Marketing, attribution and growth

Where do new users come from, which channels bring people who stay, what does a user cost to win, and is word of mouth working?

For Nutgraf

None of this applies yet: Nutgraf is private and hidden from search engines. It starts with a public page (the about.html Google already requires is a seed) and a waitlist or invitations.

Ideas to know

UTM parameters
Tags on a link that say where a visit came from: ?utm_source=linkedin&utm_medium=social&utm_campaign=launch. Every link you share gets them.
Referrer
The site the browser came from. Free, but often stripped.
Attribution
Giving a channel credit for a sign-up. First touch credits the first visit, last touch the last, multi-touch splits it. None is the truth; they are lenses.
Conversion rate
The share of visitors who sign up, and of sign-ups who activate.
CAC and LTV
Cost to win a customer, and what a customer is worth over their whole time with you. A common rule of thumb wants LTV at least 3 × CAC.
K-factor
Invites per user × the share of invites that become users. Above 1, each user brings in more than one more. Rare.
Incrementality
Would they have come anyway? Tested with holdouts: show the campaign to some regions or people and not others.
The privacy shift
Safari and Firefox block third-party cookies; iPhone apps must ask before tracking people across other companies' apps. Following individuals is less exact everywhere, so big apps lean on measurement partners and statistical models.

Area 3 of 7

Qualitative and experience analytics

Why do people do what the numbers show, and what frustrates them?

Ideas to know

Session replay
A reconstruction of a visit (not a video): taps, scrolls, screens. Shows where someone hesitated or got stuck.
Heatmaps
Where people tap and how far they scroll, summed over many visits.
Rage taps, dead taps
Repeated taps on one spot, or taps on things that do nothing. Automatic frustration signals.
Product-market fit survey
"How would you feel if you could no longer use Nutgraf?" When 40% or more say "very disappointed", the product has found its fit (the Sean Ellis test).
NPS
"How likely are you to recommend Nutgraf?" from 0 to 10. Promoters (9, 10) minus detractors (0 to 6), from −100 to 100.
CSAT
"Was this helpful?" asked about one feature.
Interviews and usability tests
Watching five people try a task finds most of the big problems.
Voice of the customer
Support messages, feedback boards and reviews, tagged by theme and counted.

Area 4 of 7

Technical, performance and crash analytics

Is it up, is it fast, and is it broken for someone right now?

Ideas to know

Logs, metrics, traces
Lines saying what happened; numbers over time; one request followed through every part it touches. Together: observability.
Real user monitoring
Speed and errors measured on real devices, not in a lab.
Percentiles
p50 is the typical visit, p95 the slow visit one in twenty sees, p99 the worst 1%. Averages hide pain.
Error-free sessions
The share of visits with no error. Mobile teams usually hold crash-free well above 99%.
Source maps
Turn the minified code in an error report back into your real file and line.
SLO and error budget
A target you set yourself (99.5% of API calls succeed over 30 days). The 0.5% allowed to fail is a budget for risky changes; when it runs out, slow down.
Synthetic monitoring
A robot that loads the site every few minutes, so you hear about an outage before a user does.
Cold start
The extra time a Lambda takes when it has not run for a while.

Area 5 of 7

Experimentation and feature flags

Did this change make things better, and can I release it safely?

Ideas to know

Feature flag
A switch read while the app runs: ship code turned off, then turn it on for you, for 10%, for everyone, without a deploy. Doubles as a kill switch.
A/B test
Half get A (control), half get B (variant). Compare a primary metric chosen in advance; guardrail metrics must not get worse.
Statistical significance
How unlikely the difference would be if B were no better. Peeking daily and stopping when it looks good makes false wins; sequential testing corrects for it.
Sample ratio mismatch
A 50/50 split that comes out 53/47 means a broken experiment. Do not trust its result.
Novelty effect
A new thing gets clicks because it is new. Let tests run long enough for that to wear off.
Holdout
A group kept on the old experience for months, to measure the long-run effect of many changes together.

Why small products rarely A/B test

Users needed in each group to notice a lift on a 20% conversion rate, at 95% confidence and 80% power:

Lift to detect Users per group
5% (20% → 21%) about 25,600
10% (20% → 22%) about 6,500
20% (20% → 24%) about 1,700
50% (20% → 30%) about 300
What works for Nutgraf now

You cannot A/B test with one user. Two things do work. Flags let you ship dark and turn a feature on for yourself, which you already do by hand. Interleaving can settle the open ranking questions, the LLM reranker (issue 69) and the model bake-off (issue 27), from your own opens and thumbs within a few weeks.

Area 6 of 7

Engagement and lifecycle messaging

Which messages bring people back, and which annoy them into leaving?

Ideas to know

Lifecycle stages
New → activated → engaged → at risk → dormant → resurrected, or gone. Each stage gets different messages.
Triggered or scheduled
Caused by behaviour ("a story you follow moved") or sent at a fixed time ("your week in Nutgraf").
Channels
Email; web push (on an iPhone only for a home-screen app, iOS 16.4 and later); messages inside the app.
Open rates are unreliable
Apple Mail loads every email's images by itself (Mail Privacy Protection), so opens look higher than they are. Count clicks and the visits that follow.
Holdout
The honest measure. Keep 10% of eligible people from getting a message; the difference in return visits is what the message caused.
Deliverability
SPF, DKIM and DMARC prove the mail is yours. Google requires bulk senders to keep spam complaints under 0.3%, recommends under 0.1%, and wants one-click unsubscribe.
Fatigue
Every message spends attention. Nutgraf exists because newsletters overwhelm you, so its own messages should be few and worth it.

Area 7 of 7

AI and LLM analytics

What does each AI feature cost, how fast is it, how often does it fail, and are its answers right?

This is where Nutgraf has the most to gain today. Claude does about ten jobs: summaries and topics, the story split, Jev's shelves, the day in brief, spotting predictions, settling them, following stories, timelines, takes, the source check with its hunt, and Ask. Titan embeds every issue, story and search.

Ideas to know

Tokens
The units models are billed in, separately for input and output. Cost is tokens × price.
Trace and span
One job from start to finish (an Ask: plan, three searches, answer), and each step in it. Shows where time and money go.
Evals
Testing a prompt or model against examples with known good answers. Offline evals run before a change ships, like unit tests; online evals score a sample of real traffic.
Golden set
A few dozen real examples with the right answers, in the repo. jev:score's labelled stories are one already.
LLM-as-judge
A second model grades answers ("is every sentence supported by its sources?"). Cheap; check it against your own judgement now and then.
Groundedness
Whether an answer is supported by the sources it cites. Its opposite is hallucination.
Human override rate
How often a person corrects the AI. Your prediction verdicts and shelf moves are exactly this.
Prompt injection
Text inside an input (a newsletter, a page the source check reads) that tries to give the model orders. Nutgraf reads untrusted text all day.

Chapter 5 · Beyond the seven

What your list was missing

Your seven cover the classic map. Seven more things decide whether the analytics are trustworthy, legal and affordable.

The data foundation

A tracking plan, naming rules, identity (one person across phone and Mac, anonymous before sign-in and known after), versioned events, tests that catch a broken event, and from Newsroom on, a warehouse as the single source of truth. Every other area stands on this.

Privacy, consent and compliance

India's DPDP Act first: its Rules were notified in November 2025; consent-manager duties start 13 November 2026, the core duties 13 May 2027. GDPR and CCPA follow if readers come from Europe or California. A privacy policy naming every tool, consent, minimum collection, retention limits, deletion on request, a data processing agreement per vendor. Plus Google's Gmail rules.

Ranking and recommendation quality

Nutgraf's core is a ranker. Precision at 5, coverage (how many newsletters ever reach the top), diversity, novelty, exploration share, and replays of past days with a new ranker before it ships. It deserves its own page once the stored score (issue 5) lands.

Cost and unit economics

AWS plus Bedrock cost per reader per month, per issue, per feature. If Nutgraf opens up, this number decides free, freemium or paid. Today the budget alert is not set.

Trust, safety and abuse

Fake sign-ups, password guessing, scraping, and above all AI cost abuse: one person scripting Ask a thousand times a day is a real bill. Measure sign-ups per hour, failed sign-ins, requests and AI spend per reader; enforce limits.

Money

If Nutgraf ever charges: trial-to-paid conversion, monthly recurring revenue, churn, revenue per reader, refunds, lifetime value. Stripe's dashboard first, then the warehouse.

The weekly review

A metric is only useful if someone looks at it. One page every week: the North Star and its inputs, the guardrails, AI cost, open alarms, three replays watched, one decision. Without this habit, every tier is decoration.

Smaller, for later: app-store analytics (only with native apps), accessibility signals (text size, reduced motion), search-engine visibility for a public site.

Tier 1 · Start now

Notebook: nearly free

One outside tool, PostHog, plus what your AWS account already offers. It touches all seven areas at a basic level.

Cost

You will produce perhaps 10,000 to 30,000 events a month, about 2% of PostHog's free million. A few CloudWatch metrics, a dashboard and some S3: a few dollars at most.

Good

One tool to learn, and its ideas (events, funnels, retention, flags) are the industry's. Nearly free. Touches every area.

Watch

PostHog is outside AWS, a change to the PRD (decision 2). Replays are capped at 5,000 a month free. One person's data is anecdote, not statistics.

If you want nothing outside AWS

The app sends events to a new POST /api/events route; Firehose writes them to S3 (about 3 cents per GB); Athena queries them ($5 per TB scanned, fractions of a cent here); CloudWatch RUM gives errors and web vitals ($1 per 100,000 events); AppConfig gives flags. The same facts and no new vendor, but no replay, no surveys and no ready-made funnels or retention charts: each is SQL you write.

Move up when a second person signs up, or events pass the free million.

Tier 2 · When Nutgraf opens up

Newsroom: a tool per desk, one warehouse

The change from Notebook: a warehouse in S3 becomes the source of truth, joining usage, AI cost and messages. Each job gets a specialist tool.

Cost at 10,000 readers

PostHog a few hundred dollars (5 cents per 1,000 events and half a cent per recording above the free allowances); Sentry from $26; Langfuse's servers roughly $100 to $250; Customer.io about $145, or Loops for less; the rest tens of dollars. About $300 to $1,500 a month. Bedrock is separate and probably bigger.

Good

The right tool for each job. The warehouse joins everything. Prompts still never leave AWS.

Watch

Five to eight vendors, each a bill, a contract and a data processing agreement. Someone has to own the tracking plan. The privacy work becomes real.

Tier 3 · For reference

Wire Service: big consumer scale

A data platform: every event collected once, streamed into a lakehouse, modelled under contracts, and fanned out to specialist tools and back into messaging.

Cost

Tool contracts from about $25,000 to well over $250,000 a month, negotiated yearly. People cost more: analytics engineers, data scientists, data engineers, growth marketers, UX researchers, reliability engineers, AI evaluators.

Why anyone pays this

At a million readers, a 1% gain in retention is worth more than the whole stack, and only this machinery can measure 1%.
